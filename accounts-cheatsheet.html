How Login Works

You type your email and password, the app checks them against your stored (hashed) credentials, and on success it hands you a session token — a wristband that keeps you logged in. MDN →
You send credentials App looks them up Store confirms a match App issues a session token
Two words people mix up: authentication = "who are you?" (login), authorization = "what are you allowed to do?" (your roles & permissions). Auth0 →

Passwords & Hashing

A good site never stores your real password — it stores a one-way, salted hash, so even a stolen database can't reveal it. OWASP →
how a password is stored
# 1. User signs up with a password
password = "hunter2"

# 2. Add a random *salt* (unique per user)
salt     = random_bytes(16)

# 3. Hash it with a slow algorithm (bcrypt/argon2)
hash     = bcrypt(password, salt)
# -> $2b$12$Nf9k...e2C  (store THIS, not the password)

# 4. Login: hash the typed password the same way,
#    then compare the two hashes.
bcrypt_verify(typed_password, stored_hash)  # true / false
▶ Plaintext → salted hash

Why it matters: hashing is one-way — you can't turn $2b$…e2C back into hunter2. The random salt means two people with the same password still get different hashes, so attackers can't reuse precomputed tables.

Real world: pick long, unique passphrases and let a password manager remember them. A 3-word phrase like purple-otter-lamp beats P@ss1.

MFA / 2FA

Multi-factor adds a second proof on top of your password: something you know (password) + something you have (a one-time code from your phone). Google 2-Step → Zoho OneAuth →
the three factor types
# A factor is a *category* of proof.
Something you KNOW   password, PIN
Something you HAVE   phone code, security key
Something you ARE    fingerprint, face

# 2FA = password + a 6-digit TOTP code
# that changes every 30 seconds:
code = TOTP(shared_secret, current_time)   # 492 118

# Authenticator apps store the shared_secret
# after you scan the setup QR code.
▶ Password, then a second factor

Why it matters: even if someone steals your password, they still can't log in without the ever-changing code on your phone. That one extra step blocks the vast majority of account takeovers.

Real world: Zoho OneAuth and Google Authenticator are authenticator apps — they generate the 6-digit codes. Prefer app codes or a security key over SMS, which can be intercepted.

IAM — Identity & Access Management

IAM decides who can do what: give a user (or group) a role, and that role carries the permissions to act on a resource. Google Cloud IAM → Zoho →
iam building blocks
# The vocabulary
Identity (principal)  a user, group, or service account
Role                  a bundle of permissions (e.g. Editor)
Permission            a single allowed action (storage.objects.get)
Policy                binds  principal + role  on a resource

# Google Cloud IAM binding (policy snippet)
{
  "role": "roles/storage.objectViewer",
  "members": ["user:asha@example.com"]
}
# Least privilege: grant the smallest role that works.
▶ A role unlocks actions

Why it matters: you grant roles, not one permission at a time — and you grant them to groups so access stays tidy as teams change. Least privilege means handing out the smallest role that still gets the job done.

Real world: in Google Cloud IAM you bind a principal to a role on a resource. In a Zoho account, admins assign roles like Admin, Agent or Billing that unlock exactly those screens.

SSO — Single Sign-On

Single Sign-On lets you log in once and then open many apps without typing your password again — one trusted key opens every door. Zoho SSO → Google Workspace →
the idea
# Without SSO: log in to each app separately
Mail   -> login
Drive  -> login
Chat   -> login

# With SSO: log in once at the identity provider,
# every connected app trusts that one session.
login @ IdP   ->  Mail  ✓
              ->  Drive ✓
              ->  Chat  ✓

# Built on protocols like SAML or OpenID Connect.
▶ One key, many apps

Why it matters: one strong login (ideally with MFA) instead of a dozen weak, reused passwords. When you leave a company, disabling that single identity instantly cuts access to every connected app.

Real world: "Sign in with Google" at work, or Okta / Zoho Directory opening Mail, Drive and chat from one login. SSO usually runs on SAML or OpenID Connect.

SAML — How It Works

SAML is the handshake behind a lot of SSO: the app (Service Provider) sends you to the Identity Provider to log in, and the IdP sends back a signed assertion the app trusts. Google SAML → Zoho →
the three parties + the assertion
# Three roles
User (browser)        wants in
SP  Service Provider  the app you're opening
IdP Identity Provider where you actually log in

# Flow
1  You open the SP
2  SP redirects you to the IdP to sign in
3  You authenticate at the IdP
4  IdP returns a *signed* SAML assertion
5  SP verifies the signature and logs you in

# A SAML assertion (trimmed XML)
<saml:Assertion>
  <saml:Subject>asha@example.com</saml:Subject>
  <ds:Signature>…IdP's signature…</ds:Signature>
</saml:Assertion>
▶ User ↔ SP ↔ IdP

Why it matters: the app never sees your password — only a signed assertion it can verify came from the trusted IdP. That signature is what stops anyone forging a "logged-in" message.

Real world: clicking "Log in with your company account" and landing on your org's Okta, Azure AD or Zoho Directory page is SAML (or its cousin OpenID Connect) at work.

OAuth — "Login with Google"

OAuth lets an app act on your behalf with a token — it gets limited access (e.g. your name & email) without ever seeing your password. Google OAuth →
the token hand-off
# 1. App sends you to Google to approve
GET accounts.google.com/o/oauth2/auth
    ?client_id=APP
    &scope=email%20profile      # what it may see
    &redirect_uri=app.com/cb

# 2. You approve on Google's own page.
# 3. Google sends an access token back to the app:
{
  "access_token": "ya29.a0Af…",
  "scope": "email profile",
  "expires_in": 3600
}
# 4. App uses the token — never your password.
▶ Token hand-off

Why it matters: you share a scoped token, not your password, and you can revoke it anytime from your account. The token also limits what the app may touch via its scope.

Real world: "Continue with Google / GitHub" buttons use OAuth. Tip: OAuth is about access (a token); when it also tells the app who you are, that layer on top is OpenID Connect.
